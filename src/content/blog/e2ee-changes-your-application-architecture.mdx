**Who this is for:** an engineer or technical founder deciding whether to add end-to-end encryption to a product that already exists, who needs to know what it costs before committing. Not a protocol explainer. The protocol is the part with good documentation.

Teams budget for the cryptography. Then they discover the cryptography was the small part.

The clearest statement of this comes from Timothy Buck, who worked on end-to-end encryption for Meta's Messenger:

> People have come to expect a large number of features in Messenger, across messaging and calling, well over 100. And each of those had to be redesigned and rebuilt. Obviously, originally they were very server-centric. **A lot of the logic was on the server and that doesn't work anymore.**
>
> — [Security Cryptography Whatever, December 2023](https://securitycryptographywhatever.com/2023/12/28/e2ee-fb-messenger/)

That is the whole article in one quote. What follows is the inventory: what stops working, what replaces it, and what the shift costs teams who have actually shipped it.

## One sentence for the whole shift

The organising idea comes from Ink & Switch's local-first essay: "The key difference between traditional systems and local-first systems is not an absence of servers, but a change in their responsibilities." ([Ink & Switch](https://www.inkandswitch.com/essay/local-first/))

In a normal application the server holds the authoritative copy and the client holds a cache. Under end-to-end encryption that inverts. The device holds the authoritative, readable copy; the server holds an encrypted replica it cannot interpret and routes envelopes between devices. Nothing about your infrastructure disappears. What changes is which side of the boundary the *work* happens on.

## What stops working, in the order you will hit it

**Week one: `WHERE content LIKE '%...%'` stops existing.** Search is the first casualty and the one users notice fastest. So do link previews, thumbnail generation, profanity filters, auto-tagging, and anything else that reads the body of a record. They have no input any more.

**Week two: push notifications go quiet.** Your push provider cannot render "Sarah: are we still on for 3pm?" because it cannot read the message. What you send instead is a contentless push that wakes the app, and the app decrypts locally and rewrites the notification — on iOS inside a `UNNotificationServiceExtension`, which is a separate process with its own keychain access, seconds of execution budget, and no access to your JavaScript runtime. Apple and Google document the pieces; almost nobody documents assembling them for a React Native app.

**Month one: the admin dashboard becomes a row count.** Support cannot "log in as user" any more — not as a policy choice, but by construction. Every internal tool that read customer content needs a different design, usually one where the user exports or shares their own data.

**Month three: moderation.** This is the single most common objection from enterprise buyers, and the answer is that moderation must be *redesigned*, not abandoned. The techniques that survive an encrypted channel are user reporting with cryptographic message franking — a construction that lets a reporting user prove to you that a specific message really was sent by a specific sender, so reports cannot be forged — plus metadata-based abuse signals like rate and fan-out. [Academic work on the problem](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04617) converges on the same two. You lose proactive content scanning; you keep reactive enforcement with better evidence than a screenshot.

**Month six: a keypair is not a user.** Your `users` table degrades into a directory of public keys, and you discover that a human being is a changing set of devices rather than an account row. Every protocol decision then has to name which device it means.

## Somebody else already published the bill

Two companies have documented the cost honestly enough to plan against.

**Zoom publishes its feature-loss list.** Zoom's support documentation for [end-to-end encryption in meetings](https://support.zoom.com/hc/en/article?id=zm_kb&sysparm_article=KB0065408) names what stops working when a host enables E2EE: "Zoom AI features, Cloud recording, Meeting chat before and after the meeting, Live streaming, Live transcription, Polling and Surveys, Zoom Apps, Zoom Notes, Zoom Whiteboard." Read that list again as what it is — most of Zoom's product-differentiation surface. Every one of those features needs a server that can see the meeting.

**Discord published the lab-versus-field gap.** On rolling out DAVE, their end-to-end encryption for voice and video: "The initial proof-of-concept worked well and DAVE's encryption functioned as expected in controlled tests. However, when we tried using DAVE in real Discord calls, it stopped working." ([Discord](https://discord.com/blog/bringing-dave-to-all-discord-platforms)) A working prototype is not evidence that you are close.

And on timelines: Meta announced the intent to make Messenger end-to-end encrypted in 2019, shipped it as the default in December 2023 — [about four and a half years](https://engineering.fb.com/2023/12/06/security/building-end-to-end-security-for-messenger/) — and was still revising the backup protocol in [May 2026](https://engineering.fb.com/2026/05/11/security/labyrinth-1-1-end-to-end-encrypted-e2ee-backups-more-reliable/), shipping Labyrinth 1.1 so messages could reach encrypted backup as they are sent rather than waiting for a device to come back online. That is a company with essentially unlimited cryptography talent, still iterating on "do messages reliably survive a lost phone" two and a half years after launch.

Your product is smaller than Messenger. The point is not the absolute duration. It is that the long pole is the application layer, not the protocol.

## The patterns that replace what you lose

The good news is that these are known problems with known shapes.

**Search moves to the device, which means you need a real local store.** Once messages are only readable on the device, the device needs an index. This is why the SDK makes `storage` a required adapter and `relay` an optional one: a local encrypted store is not an optimisation, it is where the application lives. Proton, which has invested the most in encrypted search, downloads and decrypts the mailbox and indexes it locally, and is candid that this "may take a few minutes and can be quite resource-intensive" ([Proton](https://proton.me/support/search-message-content)). Budget for that experience rather than discovering it.

**Notification content moves to the extension.** Send a wake-up, decrypt locally, rewrite the notification body. Design a sensible fallback for the case where decryption fails or the budget expires, because it will.

**Analytics moves to counts and shapes.** Envelope volume, delivery latency, error rates, session establishment failures — all still visible, all still useful. Content-derived metrics are gone.

**Sync becomes convergence rather than commitment.** Devices go offline, messages arrive out of order, and the server cannot compact what it cannot read, so the op log grows. One developer's summary after building an encrypted local-first app: "Synchronization has been my nightmare for the past couple of months. I have rewritten it a couple of times and still not fully satisfied." ([Roman Zaynetdinov](https://www.zaynetro.com/post/how-to-build-e2ee-local-first-app))

**New failure modes become first-class.** Session state must persist atomically, because a decrypt mutates the ratchet and a partial write corrupts it irrecoverably. The signature production failure is a "Bad MAC" error, and it fails *silently*: one gateway logged [a burst of 479 such exceptions](https://github.com/openclaw/openclaw/issues/14146) while believing it was connected and listening. Matrix's meta-issue for undecryptable messages [catalogues over 70 distinct causes](https://github.com/element-hq/element-meta/issues/245), and notes that debugging one generally requires logs from both the sender and the receiver. Testing encrypted failure paths is not optional polish.

**Observability has to stay outside the plaintext — and the obvious shortcut is a vulnerability.** Element shipped a bug where "the private part of the backup key pair" was written to application debug logs, affecting 156 users' key material before it was caught ([Element](https://element.io/blog/security-release-element-x-android-0-4-12-and-ios-1-6-7/)). Log envelope ids, session states, and error codes. Never log key material, and write a test that asserts you do not.

## Recovery is a day-one decision you will discover in month twelve

Users lose devices on a months-long clock, so this problem does not surface during development. It surfaces after launch, when someone's phone goes into a river and the answer is "your history is gone" and nobody agreed to that in advance.

There is no universally correct answer, and you should be suspicious of any library that implies there is. What there is, is a tradeoff with an honest statement on both sides. 1Password's security design paper puts it as directly as anyone: "Recovery mechanisms are inherently weak points in maintaining the secrecy of data." ([1Password](https://agilebits.github.io/security-design/restore.html)) Matthew Green names the slope you step onto once you build one: "Nobody is going to engineer something as complex as Signal's SVR just to store contact lists. Once you have a hammer like SVR, you're going to want to use it to knock down other nails." ([Cryptography Engineering](https://blog.cryptographyengineering.com/2020/07/10/a-few-thoughts-about-signals-secure-value-recovery/))

The useful move is to pick a named profile deliberately rather than to drift into one:

- **A server-confidential business archive** prioritises continuity. History survives device loss, multiple devices see everything, and the recovery path is a real target for coercion or account compromise. Defensible for a team collaboration product.
- **A higher-risk messaging profile** limits what is recoverable. Lose the device and lose the history; a new device starts empty. Defensible when the threat model includes device seizure.
- **A collaboration profile** sits between them, balancing offline work, membership changes, and recovery, and usually accepts more complexity than either.

The SDK gives you device-to-device transfer and encrypted backup primitives, and QR-based provisioning for linked devices. It does not silently choose the policy, because the policy is a product decision about what your users should lose, and a library that made it for you would be making it wrong for someone. [Recovery, backup, and migration](https://docs.open-e2ee.dev/build/recovery-backup-migration) compares the profiles.

## Metadata: state it, do not bury it

The most common overclaim in this category is that end-to-end encryption makes an application private. It makes the *contents* unreadable to the operator. It leaves the envelope.

Your relay still knows who sent to whom, when, how often, from which device, and roughly how large. That is a rich behavioural signal, and it is why we never claim a relay is blind. The formulation we hold ourselves to is narrower and true: **the relay never needs message plaintext or device private keys.**

Sealed sender reduces this. It does not eliminate it. Research on Signal Messenger's implementation found that "the promise of sealed sender does not compose over a conversation of messages" — with delivery receipts on by default, "Signal could link sealed sender users in as few as 5 messages" ([NDSS 2021](https://www.ndss-symposium.org/ndss-paper/improving-signals-sealed-sender/)), and [follow-up work](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09799) extended the technique to deanonymising entire group conversations. The SDK exports a `sealed-sender` module and we think you should use it; we also think its documentation should say this on the same page, which is what [limits and metadata](https://docs.open-e2ee.dev/learn/limits-and-metadata) is for.

Two more limits worth stating in the same breath. Endpoint compromise defeats all of this, because plaintext lives on the endpoint by design. And trust on first contact is trust: identity pinning detects a later key change, it does not prove who was on the other end originally.

## Is it worth it?

Sometimes not, and that is a real answer. Slack has explicitly declined end-to-end encryption, citing the cost to search, apps, and bots. Meta shut down Instagram DM encryption in May 2026, saying very few people opted in. Regulated finance, where SEC Rule 17a-4 requires retained communications, is structurally hostile without an archival story.

When it is worth it, the reason is usually one of three: a customer segment you cannot sell to otherwise, a sovereignty requirement that client-side encryption satisfies most cleanly, or the liability shift — an attacker who reaches your database gets ciphertext, and you cannot be compelled to produce what you never held. That last one is the subject of [TLS is not end-to-end encryption](/blog/tls-is-not-end-to-end-encryption/).

What should not decide it is a belief that the hard part is the cryptography. The hard part is the hundred features that assumed a server could read the data.
